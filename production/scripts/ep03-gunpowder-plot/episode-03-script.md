# The Human Story • Episode 3
# The Gunpowder Plot: The Letter That Changed the Fifth of November

Narration draft for review. Chapter times are edit targets for a 12:00 episode. Directions in italics are for the animator.

## 00:00–00:45 • A letter meant for the fire

*Close on the folds of a letter. A hand smooths it out. Follow the reader’s eyes down the page. Let the paper rustle before the narrator comes in.*

The person who wrote this letter wanted it burnt. Four hundred years later, you can still read it. There’s no name at the bottom. There’s no date at the top. But the man who received it knew exactly which appointment the writer wanted him to miss. He was due in Parliament.

Lord Monteagle could have made his excuses and stayed at home. The stranger who sent the warning would have had no reason to complain. Instead, Monteagle took the letter to a government minister. Within days, a man was in custody at Westminster. He gave his name as John Johnson. We remember him as Guy Fawkes.

## 00:45–02:05 • A new king

*Pull back from the page to a map of Scotland and England. Introduce James with both royal titles. Move into a parish street as the church bell sounds.*

Two years earlier, England had been waiting for a king. Elizabeth the First had died, and the man who inherited her throne was already a king in Scotland. To his Scottish subjects he was James the Sixth. To his English subjects he became James the First. One man now wore both crowns. Among the people watching to see what he would do were England’s Catholics.

English law required attendance at services of the established Church. Catholics who stayed away could face fines. What happened on a Sunday morning could reach into a family’s finances for years. Some Catholics hoped a change of monarch might bring relief. James had a Catholic mother, Mary, Queen of Scots, although his own upbringing and faith were Protestant.

Then came peace between England and Spain, a Catholic power. For people hoping that peace abroad might improve their position at home, the settlement brought disappointment. Catholics had to decide how to live under this king. One small circle of men decided he should die. Another Catholic would carry the warning that helped save his life.

## 02:05–03:40 • The man behind the plot

*Introduce Robert Catesby first. Add the other four names as the men arrive. Move the camera to Princess Elizabeth’s portrait when the narration turns to her.*

The man at the centre of the conspiracy was Robert Catesby, a gentleman from Warwickshire. In May sixteen hundred and four, five men met in London. There was Catesby, Thomas Winter, Thomas Percy, John Wright and Guy Fawkes. Of those five names, the one familiar to most people today belongs to the man arrested at Westminster. It was Catesby who proposed the attack.

His intended victims were the king and the people assembled for the State Opening of Parliament. Consider what that occasion meant. James would address the political nation gathered around him. Members of the Commons would come to hear him. Men who disagreed about religion, about government, even about the king himself, would be there in the ordinary course of their duties.

Catesby’s ambitions reached beyond Westminster. The conspirators hoped that Catholic gentlemen in the Midlands would rally to them. They meant to take possession of James’s young daughter Elizabeth. On paper, a royal child gave their imagined new government a claim to the throne. In life, she was a child whose father they intended to murder. And the support on which the whole enterprise depended existed largely in the conspirators’ expectations.

## 03:40–05:15 • Ten days before Parliament

*Return to Hoxton. Track the folded sheet from the footman to Monteagle. Reproduce only the words spoken from the letter, in large type. Keep the writer out of view.*

On the twenty-sixth of October, sixteen hundred and five, Lord Monteagle received the warning at his house in Hoxton, north of the City of London. A contemporary account says an unknown person delivered it to his footman. Here was a message important enough to pass to a lord, written by someone unwilling to sign it.

The writer appeals to Monteagle’s concern for his own life. Find an excuse to miss Parliament. Withdraw to the country and wait. Then comes the phrase that still catches the eye. Parliament, the letter says, will receive “a terrible blow”. It gives Monteagle enough to frighten him, and very little he could use to question the writer.

Today the sheet is held by The National Archives. We can inspect the handwriting. We can compare the text with accounts of what happened next. We still cannot put a name to the hand that wrote it. Francis Tresham, a conspirator and Monteagle’s brother-in-law, came under suspicion at the time. A family connection might explain why somebody wanted to save Monteagle. It cannot establish who sent the letter.

Monteagle took it to Robert Cecil, Earl of Salisbury, the king’s chief minister. A warning addressed to one man was now in government hands.

## 05:15–06:55 • Two names in the record

*Move from the council table to the exterior of the old Palace of Westminster. At the arrest, cut to the Commons Journal. Animate the clerk adding a marginal note.*

The king was away when the letter arrived. When he returned to London, he was told about it. Meanwhile, news of the warning had reached the conspirators. Catesby and Winter challenged Tresham. He persuaded them that he had not informed on them. On the third of November the men decided to go on. By then, the decision over whether Parliament could meet safely lay in other hands.

On the night before the opening, officials searched at Westminster. Sir Thomas Knyvett’s party found gunpowder and took a man into custody. The king, the Lords and the Commons survived. For a moment, put aside the familiar pictures of Guy Fawkes and look at what an official actually wrote.

The House of Commons met briefly on the fifth of November. Its clerk, Ralph Ewens, made a note in the margin of the Journal. The prisoner appears there as Johnson, a servant of Thomas Percy. If that single page were all we had, the name children learn on Bonfire Night would be missing from the story.

That detail tells us something about the speed of events. The clerk could record the discovery and the danger while the identity of the prisoner was still emerging. We know who Johnson was because other records survive.

## 06:55–08:25 • After the arrest

*Follow two strands. A proclamation passes from hand to hand in the Midlands. In London, successive sheets accumulate on an examiner’s desk. No imagined dialogue.*

In London, investigators wanted names. In the Midlands, Catesby had to explain to potential supporters what had happened. The king was alive. The conspiracy was public. What had sounded to the plotters like the beginning of a rising now looked very different to anyone asked to join it.

On the seventh of November, a royal proclamation declared the rebels traitors. That day, at the Tower of London, Fawkes made the first of a series of confessions. These documents are central to the story, and the circumstances in which they were obtained matter. Fawkes was tortured. A statement taken from a prisoner under such pressure has to be read alongside the other evidence.

By the eighth of November the flight had ended at Holbeach House in Staffordshire. Catesby, Percy and the Wright brothers died when local forces attacked. Others were captured. There would be no trial at which Catesby, the originator of the plot, could be questioned in public. The man who lived to be questioned, tried and condemned was Fawkes. Their different fates help explain why we have come to know one face so much better than the others.

## 08:25–10:05 • Who had to answer for it?

*Move through Westminster Hall to a parish household. Let the family hear a proclamation from the street. Treat them as illustrative people, without invented testimony.*

On the twenty-seventh of January, sixteen hundred and six, eight conspirators went on trial at Westminster Hall. The executions followed on the thirtieth and thirty-first. Fawkes died on the second day, in Old Palace Yard at Westminster. Less than three months had passed since the morning his assumed name appeared in the Commons Journal.

For English Catholics who had played no part in the conspiracy, its consequences were only beginning. When Parliament reassembled, some Protestant members demanded action against Catholics. James himself acknowledged that most were loyal. Yet he supported an oath requiring Catholics to affirm their allegiance to him and reject the Pope’s authority to depose him.

Think back to the letter. Monteagle had been faced with a threat to people of different beliefs. He had taken it to the authorities while there was still time to act. If you were looking for evidence of what a Catholic subject might do when the king was in danger, his conduct was there in the official account of the king’s deliverance.

For his fellow Catholics, that example offered little protection against suspicion. A handful of men had chosen treason. People who had never met them would be asked to prove their loyalty. The arrests ended the immediate peril to Parliament. They could do little to undo what the conspiracy had set in motion outside it.

## 10:05–12:00 • How a date became a tradition

*Match a swinging church bell to a later bell. Carry the sound across generations of the same street. End on the surviving letter, then the channel end card.*

In January sixteen hundred and six, Parliament also decided how the fifth of November should be remembered. It passed a law providing for an annual thanksgiving. Each year, congregations would hear again how the king and Parliament had been delivered. Before the anniversary became a familiar night out, it was a date on the church calendar, with a lesson that preachers were expected to draw from it.

The sound travelled beyond the church door. Bells rang. People lit bonfires, and some early celebrations included fireworks. What went into the fire changed with the times. In the reign of Charles the First, there are signs of people burning figures of the Pope or the devil. At moments of particular anxiety about a Catholic successor to the throne, such displays became more popular. The figure on the bonfire had a history of its own before it usually bore the name of Guy Fawkes.

By the nineteenth century there were chants associated with collecting money and firewood. Children could take part in the ritual without ever having heard the names of Robert Catesby or Ralph Ewens. Across generations, a complicated political story had acquired something almost any child could remember. A name. A date. A fire in the dark.

Back in the archive, the letter is quieter company. Its writer left no signature and asked that the page be destroyed. Monteagle kept it. Of all the objects that might stand for the Gunpowder Plot, perhaps the most telling is this fragile sheet of paper, still here because somebody chose to pass it on.
