# Director brief · Episode 5 · The Industrial Revolution (`ep05-industrial`)

Written 21 November 2026. **Lean mode** (`production/LEAN-MODE.md`): research, then script, then one combined QC review; YouTube keywords once at the end; MUST-only assets; one session per phase.

## Subject
The Industrial Revolution, roughly 1760–1850 in Britain and spreading through the 19th century: steam power, factories, coal and iron. It brought sustained economic growth, mass urbanisation and rising population, and also harsh working conditions (including child labour), polluted cities and the start of large-scale fossil-fuel emissions.

**Working title:** *The Industrial Revolution: The Fire That Never Went Out* (alternatives come with the YouTube pack at the end).

## Audience and tone
Ages 16–55, many with little history. Intelligent, never childish, British English. "History is made by people": tell it through people. Use real people with name tags (for example Abraham Darby, James Watt and Matthew Boulton, Richard Arkwright, George Stephenson, factory children whose testimony was recorded by parliamentary inquiries, Engels on Manchester, the Luddites, the Chartists). Fictional illustrative workers are allowed only if they are clearly labelled and never presented as real people.

## Angle
**Why Britain, why then, and who paid?** The economic leap was real and unprecedented, and it was built on coal, cotton (including slave-grown cotton from the American South), cheap labour and empire. Show the gains (incomes, life expectancy eventually rising, the railways) and the costs (child labour, cholera, slums, the hockey-stick of CO₂ emissions) without a single-cause story. Keep long-term causes (agriculture, coal geology, markets, institutions, high wages and cheap energy, colonial trade) separate from the triggers (specific inventions and investments). Present the debates as debates: why Britain; the "standard of living" debate; how far slavery and empire financed industrialisation.

## Section plan (10 chapters, 600 s; adjust to the research)
1. Cold open: a single moment (for example a steam engine starting, or a child at a mill at dawn) and the "hockey stick" of human history. ~35 s
2. Before: a world of muscle, wind, water and wood; Malthusian limits. ~50 s
3. Coal and the steam engine: Newcomen, then Watt and Boulton. ~60 s
4. Cotton and the factory: Arkwright, Cromford, the mill system; where the cotton came from. ~65 s
5. Iron, canals and railways: Darby, Stephenson, Rainhill 1829, the Liverpool & Manchester line in 1830. ~60 s
6. The city: Manchester, slums, cholera, Engels. ~65 s
7. Working lives: child labour, the Factory Acts, Luddites, Chartists, unions. ~70 s
8. The world: spread to Belgium, the US, Germany and Japan; empire and the "great divergence"; Indian textiles. ~65 s
9. The bill arrives: coal smoke, fossil fuels and CO₂, from Fourier/Tyndall's science to today (careful, sourced figures). ~60 s
10. Legacy: the world it made, and what it asks of us. ~70 s

## Sensitive topics
- **Child labour, injury and early death:** sombre, non-graphic, no comic SFX. Use recorded testimony (Sadler Committee 1832, Ashley/Children's Employment Commission 1842) quoted exactly, with its context; note that the Sadler evidence has been criticised as one-sided.
- **Slavery and cotton:** state the link plainly and accurately, with sourced figures. No imagery of violence; show its weight through objects, maps and bales of cotton.
- **Climate:** factual and sourced (for example Global Carbon Project or IPCC figures). Not alarmist, not dismissive; label estimates as estimates.
- **Colonialism and India's textile industry:** present the scholarly debate fairly.

## Visual notes
